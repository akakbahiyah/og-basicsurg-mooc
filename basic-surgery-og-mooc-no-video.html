Basic Surgery in Obstetrics & Gynaecology

A structured, self-paced course preparing Master of O&G trainees to understand, assist at, and safely perform two foundational operations: caesarean section and total abdominal hysterectomy.

Level: Early MMed (Yr 1–2) 4 modules · 26 lessons ~10–14 hours Formative + graded exam
Read before you begin

This course supports — it does not replace — supervised operative training, simulation, and your local curriculum. Technique, drugs, antibiotic and thromboprophylaxis regimens vary between hospitals and countries. Always follow your local departmental protocols and current national guidelines and operate only under appropriate supervision until signed off as competent.

What this course is

This is a Massive Open Online Course (MOOC), built to be worked through independently or in a teaching firm. It combines short readings, annotated operative step-throughs, safety checklists, image-based anatomy, worked cases, and self-marking quizzes with instant feedback. Your progress is saved automatically in your own browser.

Course aim

By the end of the course the trainee will be able to describe the indications, relevant anatomy, operative steps, complications, and post-operative care for caesarean section and abdominal hysterectomy to a standard expected of a trainee beginning to perform these procedures under supervision.

Course learning outcomes (CLOs)

On completing this course, the trainee should be able to:

  1. CLO1 — Apply the principles of safe operative practice: informed consent, the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, asepsis, patient positioning, thromboprophylaxis and antibiotic prophylaxis.
  2. CLO2 — Describe the surgical anatomy of the anterior abdominal wall and female pelvis, including the course of the ureter and the pelvic blood supply.
  3. CLO3 — List the indications, contraindications and urgency classification of caesarean section, and sequence the operative steps of a lower-segment caesarean section (LSCS).
  4. CLO4 — Recognise, prevent and outline initial management of the common intra-operative and post-operative complications of caesarean section, including major obstetric haemorrhage.
  5. CLO5 — List the indications for abdominal hysterectomy and sequence the operative steps of a total abdominal hysterectomy, with or without salpingo-oophorectomy.
  6. CLO6 — Identify strategies to prevent and manage ureteric, bladder, bowel and vascular injury during pelvic surgery.
  7. CLO7 — Reflect structurally on operative performance, maintain an operative logbook, and construct a personal learning plan for progression to independent practice.

How the course works

  • Modules & lessons — Work top-to-bottom through the sidebar. Mark a lesson complete with the button at its foot; your progress ring updates and is remembered.
  • Signature "operative note" panels — The dark green numbered panels sequence a procedure step-by-step. Surgery is an ordered task, so the numbering is the content — learn the sequence and the reason for each step.

You have completed the course when you have (1) marked all lessons complete, (2) attempted every module quiz, and (3) completed the reflective logbook in Module 3. A self-issued certificate template is provided in the final module.

A note on scope

This edition covers caesarean section and total abdominal hysterectomy. The framework is designed to expand — myomectomy, ectopic surgery, instrumental delivery, perineal repair and laparoscopic entry will be added as further modules in the future.

Module 0 · Foundations · Lesson 1

Consent, the WHO checklist & documentation

Before an incision is made, the safest surgery has already been done — in the consent conversation, the team brief and the checklist.

Lesson outcomes

Take valid informed consent for a caesarean section or hysterectomy; run the three phases of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist; and write an operation note that would satisfy a colleague, an auditor and a court.

Valid informed consent

Consent is a process, not a signature. For it to be valid the patient must have capacity, must be given sufficient information, and must decide voluntarily. Since the Montgomery standard, the test of "sufficient information" is patient-centred: you must disclose the material risks a reasonable patient in her position would want to know, and any risk this particular patient would attach significance to.

Cover, and document, the following

  • The indication and the intended benefit.
  • What the operation involves, in plain language.
  • Serious and frequently occurring risks — quantified where possible (see procedure modules for figures to use).
  • Reasonable alternatives, including conservative management and doing nothing.
  • Additional or unplanned procedures that may become necessary (e.g. blood transfusion; at caesarean: hysterectomy for uncontrollable haemorrhage; at hysterectomy: oophorectomy, bowel/bladder repair).
  • Anaesthetic implications (discussed jointly with anaesthesia).
Consent in the emergency

In a category-1 caesarean, consent may be verbal and abbreviated — the priority is the safe, and timely delivery of the baby. Document what was discussed, the time pressure, and that a full discussion was not possible. If the woman lacks capacity (e.g. eclampsia, reduced consciousness), act in her best interests under your local legal framework and involve seniors.

The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist

A short, spoken, team-based checklist that measurably reduces surgical mortality and complications. It has three phases:

◧ WHO Surgical Safety Checklist — three phases
1
Sign In — before induction of anaesthesia. Confirm identity, site, procedure, consent; check anaesthesia machine and pulse oximeter; known allergy; aspiration/airway risk; risk of major blood loss and IV access/blood availability. In O&G: confirm gestation/lie for CS, group-and-save or crossmatch status, and antacid prophylaxis.
2
Time Out — before skin incision, whole team pauses. All introduce themselves by name and role; surgeon, anaesthetist and nurse verbally confirm patient, site and procedure; anticipated critical events; antibiotic prophylaxis given in the last 60 min; VTE prophylaxis; imaging displayed. This is the single best moment to prevent wrong-patient/wrong-procedure errors and to share the surgical plan.
3
Sign Out — before the patient leaves theatre. Nurse confirms the procedure recorded; instrument, swab and sharp counts correct; specimen labelling; equipment problems; and key recovery/post-op concerns are handed over. A retained swab is a "never event" — the count is a hard stop, not a formality.

The operation note

Write it (or verify it) immediately after surgery. A good note is legible, contemporaneous and complete. Minimum content:

FieldWhat to record
HeaderDate, time, elective/emergency, urgency category
TeamOperating surgeon, assistant, anaesthetist, scrub team; who supervised/performed
ProcedureExact operation performed; indication
FindingsAnatomy, pathology, any abnormality

SpecificsEstimated blood loss; drugs given (e.g. oxytocin); tubes/drains; swab & instrument count status

Exam pearl

"EBL, drugs, counts, plan" are the four items most often missing from a trainee's op note. Build them into your habit and you will never write an incomplete note.

Module 0 · Foundations · Lesson 2

Theatre discipline, asepsis & positioning

The habits that keep an infection rate low are unglamorous and non-negotiable.

Lesson outcomes

Scrub, gown and glove aseptically; position and prepare the patient for abdominal surgery; and describe the measures that reduce surgical site infection.

Asepsis: the chain of protection

  • Surgical hand antisepsis — a full first scrub of the day (nails, then hands to elbows) using an approved agent; subsequent scrubs may be shorter. Alcohol-based surgical rubs are an accepted alternative where supported locally.
  • Gowning & gloving — closed gloving technique; consider double-gloving for procedures with sharp bone/needle exposure. Regard anything below waist level or behind you as non-sterile.
  • Skin preparation — alcohol-based chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine, applied from the intended incision outward, allowed to dry fully before draping (wet alcohol prep plus diathermy is a fire risk).
  • Hair — do not shave; clip if removal is needed, immediately before surgery.
  • Normothermia & glycaemic control — keep the patient warm and, in diabetics, well-controlled; both lower infection risk.
Surgical fire triangle

Oxygen + alcohol-based prep + diathermy spark = fire. Let skin prep dry, avoid pooling under drapes, and warn the team before activating diathermy near a fresh prep.

Positioning

  • Caesarean section — supine with a 15° left lateral tilt (or manual uterine displacement) to relieve aortocaval compression; arms may be extended for anaesthetic access; urinary catheter in situ.
  • Abdominal hysterectomy — supine (some surgeons use a modified/low lithotomy for uterine manipulation and vaginal access); slight Trendelenburg after opening improves pelvic exposure by displacing bowel cephalad.
  • Protect pressure points and the brachial plexus; secure the patient before tilt; place the diathermy return electrode over well-perfused muscle.

Bladder management

An empty bladder is out of harm's way and improves the operative field. Insert an indwelling urinary catheter after anaesthesia for both procedures. In caesarean the full bladder is pushed down within the bladder flap; in hysterectomy the bladder is dissected off the cervix — in both, a distended bladder is easier to injure.

Exam pearl

The three commonest lapses examiners probe: prep not dry before draping, tilt omitted at CS (→ maternal hypotension and fetal compromise), and catheter forgotten. Name all three unprompted and you look like someone who has scrubbed for real.

Instruments, energy sources, sutures & tissue handling

You cannot ask for what you cannot name, and you cannot close what you do not understand.

Lesson outcomes

Name the common instruments of open pelvic surgery, use diathermy safely, and select an appropriate suture and needle for a given tissue.

Core instrument set

GraspingToothed (Lane's) & non-toothed (DeBakey) forceps; Littlewood, Allis; sponge-holderToothed for skin/fascia; non-toothed for delicate/bowel

Energy sources

  • Monopolar diathermy — current passes from the active electrode through the patient to a return plate. Cutting = continuous waveform (vaporises); coagulation = interrupted waveform (dries/seals). High power and precision but higher lateral thermal spread.
  • Bipolar diathermy — current passes only between the two limbs of the forceps; no return plate, minimal spread. Safer near ureter, bowel and in the presence of pacemakers.
  • Vessel-sealing / advanced bipolar & ultrasonic devices — seal and divide pedicles; increasingly used but always know the open clamp-cut-tie technique first.
Diathermy safety

Common harms: return-plate burns (poor contact), capacitive coupling, direct coupling to a metal instrument, and inadvertent activation. Keep the active electrode in its quiver when not in use, use the lowest effective power, and never activate near bowel or ureter without a clear view.

Sutures

Choose by absorbability, structure (mono- vs multifilament), and size (larger number = thinner thread).

SutureTypeTypical O&G use
Polyglactin 910 (Vicryl)Absorbable, braidedUterus, vaginal vault/cuff, rectus sheath, subcutaneous, pedicle ties
Poliglecaprone (Monocryl)Absorbable, monofilamentSubcuticular skin closure
Polydioxanone (PDS)Absorbable, monofilament, long-lastingMass closure of fascia in some settings
Polypropylene (Prolene)/NylonNon-absorbable, monofilamentInterrupted skin sutures, drain fixation

Needles are usually round-bodied for soft tissue/muscle/uterus (spreads fibres) and cutting for skin. Hold the needle roughly two-thirds from the tip and pass it following its curve.

Tissue handling & Halsted's principles

Gentle handling heals; rough handling bleeds and infects. The enduring Halsted principles: gentle tissue handling, meticulous haemostasis, preservation of blood supply, strict asepsis, minimal tension, accurate approximation, and obliteration of dead space.

Exam pearl

If asked "what suture and why," answer with all three axes: material, structure, size — e.g. "1 polyglactin, an absorbable braided suture, to close the rectus sheath with a continuous non-locking technique."

Module 0 · Foundations · Lesson 4

Surgical anatomy of the abdominal wall & female pelvis

Every complication in this course is, at root, an anatomical one. Learn the layers and the ureter now, and the operations become logical.

Lesson outcomes

List the layers of the anterior abdominal wall traversed at laparotomy; describe the blood supply of the uterus and adnexa; and state the course of the ureter and where it is at risk.

Layers of the anterior abdominal wall

At a midline or Pfannenstiel/Joel-Cohen incision you pass, in order, through:

Landmark

Below the arcuate line (~midway between umbilicus and pubis) there is no posterior rectus sheath — the rectus lies directly on transversalis fascia. The inferior epigastric vessels run laterally behind the rectus and can be injured if a transverse incision is extended too far laterally.

Blood supply of the uterus & adnexa

  • Uterine artery — a branch of the anterior division of the internal iliac (hypogastric) artery. It reaches the uterus at the level of the internal os, running in the base of the broad ligament (cardinal ligament).
  • Ovarian artery — arises directly from the aorta, descends in the infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament to the ovary, and anastomoses with the uterine artery.
  • These two supplies anastomose, which is why ligating one does not devascularise the uterus and why control of both pedicles is needed at hysterectomy.

The ureter — the surgeon's constant concern

The ureter is retroperitoneal. Three surgical danger zones in pelvic surgery:

  1. Pelvic brim — the ureter crosses the bifurcation of the common iliac artery; at risk when clamping the infundibulopelvic ligament.
  2. Under the uterine artery — it passes ~1.5 cm lateral to the cervix, beneath the uterine artery ("water under the bridge"); at risk when clamping the uterine pedicle.
  3. Near the vaginal angle / uterosacral ligament — as it turns medially into the bladder trigone; at risk when securing the cardinal ligament and vaginal angles.
The rule that prevents ureteric injury

See it, or move it. Before clamping any pedicle near the ureter, either directly visualise the ureter or skeletonise and reflect it laterally/away. Staying close to the uterus ("hugging the uterus") when taking the uterine and cardinal pedicles keeps you medial to the ureter.

The pelvic ligaments (what you will clamp)

Module 0 · Foundations · Lesson 5

Haemostasis, thromboprophylaxis & antibiotic prophylaxis

Three quiet interventions that prevent the three commonest post-operative disasters: bleeding, clot and sepsis.

Lesson outcomes

Describe methods of achieving surgical haemostasis; state the principles of peri-operative VTE risk assessment and prophylaxis; and give the correct timing and choice of surgical antibiotic prophylaxis for CS and hysterectomy.

Haemostasis

Bleeding is controlled by one or a combination of:

  • Mechanical — direct pressure/packing, clamp-and-tie of a pedicle, suture ligation, clips, and definitive vessel ligation.
  • Thermal — mono-/bipolar diathermy, vessel-sealing devices, argon.
  • Chemical/adjunctive — topical haemostatic agents; systemic tranexamic acid; and correction of coagulopathy (the obstetric priority — treat the cause, warm the patient, and replace clotting factors early).

The reliable sequence when faced with bleeding: pressure first to buy time and vision, identify the source, then apply definitive control. Blind clamping in a pool of blood injures ureter and bowel.

Topical haemostatic agents (adjuncts)

When a raw surface or pedicle oozes and cannot simply be tied, a topical agent can help. These supplement surgical haemostasis — they never replace a ligature on a named vessel, and most work poorly if the patient is coagulopathic, hypothermic or acidotic. Availability and choice follow your local protocol; common examples:

Agent (example)What it is made ofHow it helps stop bleeding
Oxidised regenerated cellulose (e.g. Surgicel)Sterilised plant cellulose woven into a mesh/gauzeGives a physical matrix for clot and swells to tamponade the surface; its low pH also helps denature surface blood proteins and is locally bactericidal. Use the minimum needed and remove excess — it swells and can act as a foreign body.
Absorbable gelatin (e.g. Spongostan, Gelfoam)Purified porcine (or bovine) gelatin spongeAbsorbs many times its weight in blood and provides a scaffold and tamponade on which platelets aggregate and clot forms. Carries no active clotting agent itself.
Microfibrillar collagen (e.g. Avitene)Purified bovine collagen (powder or sheet)Collagen is a natural trigger for platelet adhesion and aggregation, accelerating the platelet plug on contact.
Thrombin–gelatin matrix (e.g. Floseal)Gelatin granules combined with thrombin, as a flowable pasteCombines a mechanical matrix with active thrombin, which converts the patient's fibrinogen to fibrin — useful for irregular, oozing surfaces.

Exam pearl

Group them by mechanism. Passive / matrix agents (cellulose, gelatin, collagen) supply only a scaffold and rely on the patient's own clotting to work; active agents (thrombin matrix, fibrin sealant) supply clotting factors and work even when the patient's own cascade is impaired. None substitutes for ligating a bleeding named vessel or for correcting coagulopathy.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis

Pregnancy and pelvic surgery are both prothrombotic; a caesarean roughly quadruples VTE risk versus vaginal birth. Every surgical patient should have a documented VTE risk assessment. Prophylaxis combines:

  • Mechanical — anti-embolism stockings and/or intermittent pneumatic compression, early mobilisation, hydration.
  • Pharmacological — low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), dosed by weight and risk, usually started after surgery when haemostasis is secure and continued for a duration set by risk (a short course after uncomplicated surgery; up to 6 weeks postpartum for high-risk obstetric patients). Follow your local/national guideline (e.g. RCOG Green-top on thromboprophylaxis) for exact drugs, doses and timing.
Regional anaesthesia + LMWH

Mind the interval between LMWH and insertion/removal of a spinal/epidural catheter to avoid spinal haematoma. This is coordinated with anaesthesia — never give a prophylactic dose without checking the neuraxial plan.

Antibiotic prophylaxis

Exam pearl — the timing switch

For CS, the evidence-based change is giving antibiotics before incision rather than after cord clamping. Know this and the reason (lower maternal endometritis/wound infection, no increase in neonatal problems). Actual drug choice is local — quote the principle, then "as per departmental protocol."

Module 1 · Caesarean Section · Lesson 1

Indications, contraindications & urgency classification

Knowing why and how fast shapes every decision that follows — anaesthesia, incision, and who you call.

Lesson outcomes

Classify the indications for caesarean section, apply the urgency (category 1–4) classification, and describe the Robson Ten-Group Classification System for audit.

What a caesarean section is

Delivery of the fetus through incisions in the abdominal wall and uterus. The vast majority are lower-segment caesarean sections (LSCS) through a transverse incision in the thinned lower uterine segment — safer to heal and less prone to rupture in a future pregnancy than the classical (upper-segment vertical) incision.

Indications

CategoryExamples
MaternalPrevious CS (esp. ≥2, or classical), maternal request after counselling, some maternal medical conditions, obstructing pelvic mass
FetalNon-reassuring fetal status / suspected compromise, malpresentation (breech, transverse lie), certain multiple pregnancies
PlacentalPlacenta praevia, placenta accreta spectrum, significant abruption
Mechanical / labourFailure to progress / cephalopelvic disproportion, failed instrumental delivery, cord prolapse
InfectiveTo reduce vertical transmission in selected infections (e.g. primary genital herpes at term, high maternal HIV viral load)

There are few absolute contraindications; caesarean is usually the alternative to a riskier vaginal birth. Relative cautions include a dead fetus or lethal anomaly (where vaginal birth avoids maternal surgery if safe) and severe maternal coagulopathy that should be corrected first if time allows.

Urgency classification (the four categories)

A shared language between obstetrics, anaesthesia and theatres. Widely used (RCOG/NICE):

◧ Urgency categories
1
Immediate threat to life of mother or fetus (e.g. cord prolapse with bradycardia, uterine rupture, prolonged fetal bradycardia, scalp pH-confirmed acidosis). Aim: deliver as fast as safely possible — the classic 30-minute target for decision-to-delivery is an audit standard, not a deadline; sometimes you must be faster.
2
Maternal or fetal compromise, not immediately life-threatening (e.g. failure to progress with a pathological CTG). Urgent but allows a brief window; often within ~75 minutes by audit convention.
3
No compromise but needs early delivery (e.g. failed induction, ruptured membranes needing delivery). Booked onto the next available list.
4
Elective — planned to suit the woman and the service. e.g. previous CS with maternal choice, term breech.
Category is dynamic

A category-3 can become a category-1 in seconds (e.g. sudden bradycardia). State the current category out loud so the whole team scales its response — anaesthetic technique in particular depends on it.

Robson Ten-Group Classification System (TGCS)

The WHO-endorsed standard for auditing and comparing caesarean rates. It sorts every birth into one of ten mutually exclusive groups using routinely collected variables: parity, previous CS, onset of labour (spontaneous / induced / pre-labour CS), gestation, fetal presentation (cephalic, breech, transverse/oblique) and number of fetuses. It doesn't tell you whether an individual CS was right — it lets a unit see which groups drive its rate and benchmark against others.

Exam pearl

Distinguish the two classifications examiners love to muddle: urgency categories 1–4 describe how fast to deliver one woman; the Robson TGCS is a population audit tool. They answer different questions.

Module 1 · Caesarean Section · Lesson 2

Preoperative preparation & anaesthesia

The five minutes before knife-to-skin decide how the next hour goes.

Lesson outcomes

Prepare a woman for caesarean section, including consent, investigations and prophylaxis; and describe the anaesthetic options and their implications for the surgeon.

Preoperative preparation checklist

  • Consent — indication, procedure, material risks (see below), alternatives, possible additional procedures (transfusion, and rarely hysterectomy for haemorrhage).
  • Bloods — full blood count; group-and-save, with crossmatch if higher bleeding risk (praevia/accreta, multiple previous CS, anaemia).
  • Antacid prophylaxis — H₂-antagonist ± prokinetic and non-particulate antacid to reduce aspiration risk.
  • Fasting where elective; in an emergency, treat as a full stomach.
  • IV access, fluids, and blood availability appropriate to risk.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis before incision; VTE risk assessment; catheter; left lateral tilt.
  • Neonatal team alerted, resuscitaire checked; oxytocic drawn up and ready.
  • WHO checklist — Sign In and Time Out completed.

Risks to quote at consent (typical order of magnitude)

Use figures your unit audits; commonly cited magnitudes for counselling:

Frequent risksSerious but less frequent
Persistent wound/abdominal discomfort; repeat CS likely in future pregnancies; longer recovery than vaginal birth; increased bleeding vs vaginal birthEmergency hysterectomy; need for further surgery; ICU admission; bladder or ureteric injury; VTE; and, in future pregnancies, placenta praevia/accreta and uterine rupture

To the baby: a small risk of laceration at delivery. Balance all against the risks of not operating.

Anaesthesia — and why it matters to the surgeon

TechniqueNotes for the operating surgeon
Spinal (regional) — most commonAwake mother, best neonatal outcomes, good post-op analgesia. Rapid onset. Preferred wherever time allows, including many category-1 cases if a block is already in place.

Under GA, do not start until the anaesthetist confirms the airway is secured — but be fully prepared so there is no delay. Announce estimated blood loss and call for oxytocin promptly after delivery. Silence in the room is a failure of teamwork, not calmness.

Module 1 · Caesarean Section · Lesson 3

Step-by-step: lower-segment caesarean section

Learn the sequence and the reason for each step. Reasons make you safe when anatomy is distorted.

Lesson outcomes

Sequence the operative steps of an LSCS from skin to skin, justify each step, and state the evidence behind the modern (blunt-entry) technique.

Choice of skin incision

  • Pfannenstiel — a low, gently curved transverse incision ~2–3 cm above the symphysis; sharp dissection of layers.
  • Joel-Cohen / Misgav-Ladach — a straight transverse incision slightly higher, with blunt (finger) extension of most layers. Cochrane/CORONIS evidence associates blunt-entry techniques with less blood loss, shorter operating time and less post-operative pain. This is the modern default in many units.
  • Midline vertical — reserved for extreme urgency, very poor access, or when wide exposure is needed (e.g. suspected accreta); faster entry but more pain and hernia risk.

The operation, step by step

Every step below carries its justification. Evidence marks a recommendation supported by randomised trials or systematic reviews (e.g. CORONIS, Cochrane); Rationale marks accepted anatomical or surgical reasoning used where high-quality trial evidence is limited or absent.

◧ LSCS — operative sequence (skin to skin)
1
Skin incision. Transverse suprapubic (Pfannenstiel or Joel-Cohen). Evidence: transverse incisions cause less postoperative pain and give a better cosmetic result than vertical ones; the Joel-Cohen (straight, slightly higher) incision with blunt entry is linked in trials to shorter operating time, less blood loss and less febrile morbidity than Pfannenstiel.
2
Subcutaneous fat & rectus sheath. Deepen; incise the rectus sheath transversely and extend — sharply (Pfannenstiel) or by blunt lateral traction (Joel-Cohen). Evidence: blunt (finger) extension follows natural tissue planes and is associated with less blood loss and faster entry than sharp dissection.
3
Rectus muscles. Separate in the midline and retract laterally — usually bluntly, no need to cut them. Rationale (anatomical): the recti are paired midline muscles that can be parted rather than divided, preserving strength and reducing bleeding; the inferior epigastric vessels lie laterally and are spared by staying in the midline.
4
Peritoneum. Open the parietal peritoneum high (superiorly), lift a fold, confirm no bowel/bladder, then extend. Rationale (anatomical): the bladder sits at the lower limit of the peritoneal reflection, so opening high — after tenting a fold to confirm no adherent bowel or bladder — avoids injury, particularly when previous surgery has drawn the bladder upward.
5
Identify the lower segment & reflect the bladder. Incise the loose uterovesical (visceral) peritoneum, create a bladder flap and gently push the bladder down with a Doyen retractor. Rationale (consensus): reflecting the bladder moves it clear of the uterine incision and the delivering head. Routine bladder-flap formation has limited trial evidence and some studies question it, but it remains standard practice where the lower segment is poorly formed or the bladder is adherent — follow your unit's practice.
6
Uterine incision. A small transverse incision in the lower segment with the scalpel; deepen carefully until membranes bulge, then extend laterally and slightly cephalad with the fingers (or curved scissors). Evidence: expanding the uterine incision bluntly with the fingers produces fewer unintended extensions and less blood loss than cutting with scissors; curving the expansion upward and laterally keeps it away from the uterine vessels at the angles.
7
Deliver the fetus. Deliver the presenting part (a hand behind the head, or forceps if needed) with fundal pressure from the assistant; deliver by flexion, then the shoulders and body. Rationale (technique): controlled, well-flexed, unhurried delivery avoids sudden lateral tearing into the uterine angles and their vessels.
8
Cord & oxytocic. Double-clamp and cut the cord (delayed clamping if the baby is well and not compromised); anaesthetist gives the oxytocic (e.g. oxytocin) to contract the uterus. Evidence: a prophylactic uterotonic after delivery reduces postpartum haemorrhage (oxytocin first-line, dose/route per local protocol); deferring cord clamping when the baby is well improves neonatal iron stores.
9
Deliver the placenta by controlled cord traction. Await signs of separation; avoid routine manual removal. Evidence: placental delivery by controlled cord traction rather than manual removal is associated with less blood loss and less endometritis (Cochrane).
10
Check the cavity & identify the angles. Ensure the cavity is empty; secure both angles of the uterine incision first. Rationale (anatomical): the lateral angles overlie the uterine vessels and are the classic site of brisk intra-operative bleeding, so securing them first controls the main supply.
11
Close the uterus. Continuous suture (commonly absorbable braided) in one or two layers; many units use a double layer. Exteriorising the uterus for repair is optional and surgeon-dependent. Evidence limited: single- vs double-layer closure is not settled for future scar integrity/rupture risk — many units use a double layer for possible benefit; exteriorised vs in-situ repair shows no clear safety difference. Follow your unit's practice.
12
Haemostasis & check adnexa. Confirm the suture line is dry; inspect tubes and ovaries. Rationale (surgical principle): confirming a dry suture line and inspecting the adnexa before closing prevents a return to theatre for reactionary haemorrhage.
13
Close the abdomen. Peritoneum (visceral & parietal) is usually left unclosed; reapproximate rectus muscles if needed; close the rectus sheath with a continuous suture; close subcutaneous fat if the layer is thick (>~2 cm); close skin (subcuticular or staples). Evidence: leaving the peritoneum unclosed shortens operating time without increasing adhesions or morbidity, and closing subcutaneous fat when it is ≥2 cm deep reduces wound breakdown/seroma (Cochrane/CORONIS). Rationale: the sheath is the layer that provides wound strength.
14
Final checks. Vaginal check for clots/ongoing loss and to confirm the uterus is contracted; confirm swab, instrument and sharps counts; ensure catheter draining; document EBL, drugs and plan. Rationale (safety standard): the swab/instrument count is a mandatory stop — a retained swab is a "never event" — and the vaginal check detects an atonic, bleeding uterus before the woman leaves theatre.
Evidence base — the CORONIS & Cochrane picture

Large trials and systematic reviews (e.g. the CORONIS international trial and Cochrane reviews of CS technique) support: blunt abdominal and uterine entry (less blood loss/time), non-closure of the peritoneum, spontaneous placental delivery by cord traction rather than manual removal, and antibiotics before incision. Single- vs double-layer uterine closure and exteriorisation remain areas of ongoing debate rather than settled dogma.

Exam pearl — the three "up/high" rules

Open the peritoneum high (away from bladder), extend the uterine incision cephalad and curved (away from vessels), and secure the angles first. These three habits prevent the most common trainee errors.

Difficult caesarean & intra-operative complications

The competent surgeon is defined less by the routine case than by what they do when it goes wrong.

Lesson outcomes

Anticipate and manage difficult delivery, angle extension, bladder/ureteric injury and post-partum haemorrhage at caesarean, using a stepwise escalation.

The difficult delivery of the head

  • Deeply impacted head (full dilatation CS) — the head is jammed in the pelvis. Options: push from below by an assistant (with clean technique), reverse breech extraction ("pull" the feet up and deliver as a breech), or use of a fetal pillow/tocolysis. Anticipate — extension of the uterine incision downward risks tearing the cervix, vagina and bladder.
  • Floating high head / preterm — the lower segment may be poorly formed; consider where to place the incision and be gentle to avoid trauma.

Extension of the uterine angle ("angle extension")

A tear running laterally/inferiorly into the broad ligament or downward toward the cervix and vessels. Recognise it by brisk bleeding from the angle.

Managing an angle extension

Get exposure and an assistant. Identify the apex of the tear beyond the bleeding point and place your first suture there. Beware the ureter, which runs close to a downward-lateral extension — visualise or reflect it before deep suturing. Do not blindly clamp in a bloody field.

Bladder & ureteric injury

  • Highest risk with adhesions from previous caesareans, when the bladder is adherent high on the lower segment, and with downward angle extensions.
  • Recognise bladder injury by clear fluid in the field, visible catheter/Foley balloon, or gas in the drainage bag. Repair in two layers with absorbable suture, confirm integrity (e.g. instil dye), and leave the catheter for a period per local protocol; involve urology/senior for complex or ureteric injury.

Post-partum haemorrhage (PPH) at caesarean

Stepwise escalation for atonic PPH not responding to drugs

Exam pearl

For any PPH question, structure your answer as parallel tracks: call for help + resuscitate (ABC, IV access, fluids/blood, tranexamic acid, keep warm) and simultaneously stop the bleeding (4 T's → mechanical → surgical). Sequential thinking loses marks and, in reality, lives.

Special scenarios

The cases you must recognise before you scrub, because they change the plan, the team and the incision.

Lesson outcomes

Modify caesarean planning and technique for placenta praevia and accreta spectrum, malpresentation, preterm birth, and caesarean at full dilatation.

Placenta praevia & placenta accreta spectrum (PAS)

Standard definitions (RCOG Green-top 27a/27b; consistent with ACOG and FIGO):

  • Low-lying placenta — the placental edge lies <20 mm from the internal cervical os on transvaginal ultrasound in the second or third trimester.
  • Placenta praevia — the placenta lies directly over the internal os. (These two terms have replaced the older grade I–IV / "minor–major" classification.)
  • Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) — abnormally adherent or invasive placenta, graded by depth: accreta (villi attach to myometrium with absent decidua), increta (villi invade into the myometrium), and percreta (villi penetrate through the serosa, sometimes into adjacent organs such as the bladder).

PAS is strongly associated with previous caesarean(s) together with an anterior low-lying placenta or praevia, and the risk rises with the number of prior caesareans.

  • Antenatal diagnosis. Localise the placenta with ultrasound and assess for PAS using greyscale and colour-Doppler transvaginal scanning, with MRI as an adjunct for depth or bladder/parametrial involvement. Evidence (RCOG GTG 27b): antenatal diagnosis with planned multidisciplinary care reduces maternal haemorrhage and morbidity compared with PAS first discovered during surgery.
  • Plan as an elective, senior-led procedure in a unit equipped for major haemorrhage — consultant obstetrician and anaesthetist, cross-matched blood, cell salvage where available, and urology/interventional radiology on standby. Evidence: managing PAS in a specialist centre with an experienced multidisciplinary team is associated with less blood loss and fewer complications.
  • Choose an incision that avoids the placenta. For an anterior praevia/PAS, consider a higher (classical) or otherwise placenta-sparing uterine incision to reach the fetus without cutting through the placenta. Rationale: incising through placenta provokes heavy fetal and maternal blood loss.
  • Confirmed significant accreta. The safest strategy is often caesarean hysterectomy with the placenta left in situ — do not attempt to peel an adherent placenta. Evidence / consensus (RCOG & ACOG): attempting forced removal provokes torrential haemorrhage; leaving the placenta in situ reduces blood loss.
  • Timing of planned birth is individualised and set by your local protocol — commonly around 36–37 weeks for uncomplicated praevia and earlier (≈35–36+6 weeks) for PAS, or sooner if bleeding. Guideline basis (RCOG/ACOG): planned late-preterm/early-term birth balances the risk of unscheduled haemorrhage against prematurity.
The single most important habit

In any woman with a previous caesarean and an anterior low-lying placenta, actively look for accreta antenatally. An unrecognised accreta discovered at an unplanned emergency CS at night is a preventable disaster.

Malpresentation & transverse/oblique lie

Definitions: malpresentation = any non-vertex presenting part (e.g. breech, brow, face, shoulder); transverse / oblique lie = the fetal long axis is not aligned with the long axis of the uterus.

  • Breech — deliver by breech extraction through the uterine incision; keep the fetus flexed; avoid excessive traction. Rationale: keeping the head flexed and avoiding traction prevents head entrapment and fetal injury.
  • Transverse lie with a poorly formed lower segment — a low transverse uterine incision may not give room; a vertical (classical or De Lee low-vertical) uterine incision is sometimes needed for safe delivery. Rationale: a vertical incision gives access when the lower segment is unformed; because it carries a higher risk of scar rupture in a future pregnancy, it must be documented and it changes future delivery counselling (generally repeat caesarean).

Preterm caesarean

The lower segment may be unformed and the fetus fragile. Handle gently, consider a wider or vertical incision if access is inadequate, and coordinate with neonatology. Evidence: antenatal corticosteroids reduce neonatal respiratory distress and mortality, and magnesium sulphate given before birth at early gestations provides fetal neuroprotection; defer cord clamping if the baby is stable. Exact gestational thresholds and regimens follow your local protocol.

Caesarean at full dilatation

Full-dilatation (second-stage) caesarean = a caesarean performed at full cervical dilatation. It is technically harder: the head is deeply impacted and the lower segment is stretched thin, so the uterine incision is easily placed too low (into the cervix/vagina) and extensions are common.

  • Take a moment to identify the true lower segment before incising.
  • Anticipate a difficult head: plan for push-up from below or reverse breech extraction. Evidence: comparative studies associate reverse (abdominal) breech extraction with fewer uterine extensions and less maternal morbidity than pushing the impacted head up from below.
  • Expect and be ready to repair extensions; check the bladder and vaginal fornices afterwards.
Exam pearl

"Previous CS + anterior praevia = think accreta = senior, blood, plan." And "second-stage CS = impacted head + low incision risk." These two associations answer a large share of viva questions on difficult caesareans.

Postoperative care & enhanced recovery

Recovery is a designed process, not a period of waiting.

Immediate post-operative care

  • Monitoring — observations (including uterine tone and lochia) with an early-warning score; watch for concealed bleeding.
  • Analgesia — multimodal: regional (intrathecal/epidural opioid or a transversus abdominis plane block) plus regular paracetamol and an NSAID (if no contraindication), with opioids as rescue.
  • Fluids & feeding — early oral intake is encouraged in uncomplicated cases (enhanced recovery).
  • Catheter — remove early once mobile and the block has worn off.
  • VTE prophylaxis — mechanical from the outset; pharmacological per risk assessment.
  • Support — early skin-to-skin and breastfeeding support; the operation is also a birth.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) principles

Applied to caesarean, ERAS bundles include: avoiding prolonged fasting and giving carbohydrate loading where appropriate; antibiotics before incision; normothermia; multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia; early feeding, early catheter removal and early mobilisation; and structured discharge planning. The aim is a faster, safer recovery and shorter stay without increasing readmission.

Module 1 · Caesarean Section · Applied case

Case: the category-1 caesarean

Work through the scenario, then reveal the expert reasoning.

Scenario

A 32-year-old G3P1 (one previous caesarean) is in spontaneous labour at 39 weeks. The CTG shows a prolonged deceleration to 70 bpm for 4 minutes with no recovery. On examination the cervix is 6 cm dilated and there is fresh vaginal bleeding. The registrar declares a category-1 caesarean.

1. What is your immediate priority and category, and why?

This is a category-1 caesarean — an immediate threat to fetal (and possibly maternal) life. The combination of a previous caesarean, labour, a prolonged deceleration and fresh bleeding raises the spectre of uterine rupture or abruption. Priority: call the team (senior obstetrician, senior anaesthetist, neonatology, theatre, blood), move to theatre without delay, and resuscitate the mother in parallel (IV access, fluids, blood available).

2. What anaesthetic is likely, and how should you prepare?

With true category-1 urgency and no working regional block, general anaesthesia with rapid-sequence induction is likely. Be scrubbed, prepped and draped so you can incise the moment the airway is secured — every minute counts. If a rapid spinal is feasible and the team judges it safe, that may be used; the decision is shared with anaesthesia.

3. What intra-operative findings would you specifically look for?

On entry, look for haemoperitoneum, a uterine scar dehiscence/rupture, and the state of the placenta (abruption). If the uterus is ruptured, deliver the fetus, control bleeding, and repair or, if uncontrollable, proceed to hysterectomy with senior support. Anticipate PPH and have uterotonics and the major haemorrhage protocol ready.

4. What are the key post-operative concerns?

Haemorrhage (tone, trauma, coagulopathy), the neonate's condition after an acute hypoxic insult, VTE prophylaxis once haemostasis is secure, analgesia, and a careful debrief with the parents. Document the sequence and timings meticulously.

Teaching point

The examinable thread here is parallel action: resuscitation, delivery and communication happen together, not one after the other. Name the differential (rupture/abruption) early — it changes what you expect on opening the abdomen.

Indications, types & preoperative assessment

Lesson outcomes

Types by extent and route

By route: abdominal (open — this course), vaginal, or laparoscopic/robotic. Route is chosen by uterine size, pathology, access, prior surgery and surgeon skill; minimal-access and vaginal routes generally offer faster recovery when feasible.

Common indications for benign abdominal hysterectomy

  • Symptomatic uterine fibroids (bulk symptoms, heavy menstrual bleeding) — often the reason the abdominal route is chosen, due to uterine size.
  • Heavy menstrual bleeding unresponsive to medical/conservative treatment.
  • Adenomyosis, chronic pelvic pain from defined pathology.
  • Endometriosis with severe disease (usually as part of wider surgery).
  • Uterovaginal prolapse (though the vaginal route is often preferred).
  • Malignancy / pre-malignancy — endometrial or ovarian cancer, complex atypical hyperplasia (often with BSO and staging — specialist-led).
  • Obstetric emergency — peripartum hysterectomy for uncontrollable haemorrhage or accreta (covered in Module 1).

Preoperative assessment & counselling

  • Confirm the indication and that conservative/medical/less-invasive options have been considered.
  • Fertility & hormones — hysterectomy ends fertility; discuss ovarian conservation. Removing the ovaries in a pre-menopausal woman causes surgical menopause — counsel on symptoms and HRT.
  • Cervix decision — total vs subtotal; if the cervix is retained, cervical screening must continue.
  • Investigations — FBC (correct anaemia pre-op where possible), group-and-save/crossmatch, imaging to define uterine size and pathology, endometrial assessment if abnormal bleeding, and up-to-date cervical screening.
  • Optimise — treat anaemia, optimise comorbidities; consider pre-treatment to shrink fibroids where appropriate.
  • Consent — including risks of ureteric/bladder/bowel injury, haemorrhage/transfusion, VTE, infection, conversion of subtotal plan, need for oophorectomy, and altered bladder/bowel/sexual function.
Exam pearl

Two decisions define the operation and must be documented at consent: cervix in or out (total vs subtotal) and ovaries in or out (conserve vs BSO). Examiners expect you to justify each for the individual woman — age, pathology, menopausal status and her wishes.

Applied anatomy & protecting the ureter

Why the ureter is injured at hysterectomy

Recall its three danger zones (Module 0): the pelvic brim (IP ligament), under the uterine artery ("water under the bridge"), and at the vaginal angle. Injury is more likely with distorted anatomy — large fibroids, endometriosis, a broad-ligament fibroid displacing the ureter, previous surgery, or bleeding that tempts blind clamping.

Three techniques that protect the ureter

1. Direct visualisation — open the retroperitoneum and see the ureter peristalse over the pelvic brim before taking the IP.
2. Hug the uterus — keep clamps on the uterine and cardinal pedicles close to the uterine wall, staying medial to the ureter.
3. Skeletonise & reflect — clear the uterine vessels and push the bladder (and with it the ureter's lower course) down and away before clamping. In difficult cases, place ureteric stents pre-operatively.

Blood supply reminder

The uterus is supplied by the uterine artery (from the internal iliac) and the ovarian artery (from the aorta), which anastomose. Securing pedicle 2 (ovarian) and pedicle 4 (uterine) on both sides is what devascularises the uterus — after which the cardinal and uterosacral pedicles are relatively avascular.

Exam pearl

If asked "how do you avoid ureteric injury," never answer only "be careful." Give the three concrete techniques above, and name the three anatomical zones. That is the difference between a pass and a distinction.

Step-by-step: total abdominal hysterectomy

Six pairs of pedicles, taken in order, close to the uterus. Master the sequence and the operation becomes rhythmic and safe.

Lesson outcomes

Sequence a total abdominal hysterectomy (± BSO) from incision to closure, justify each step, and describe how ovarian conservation changes step 3.

Access

Choose a Pfannenstiel/transverse incision for most benign cases; use a midline vertical incision for a very large uterus, suspected malignancy, or where wide access is needed. After opening, place the woman in slight Trendelenburg, pack the bowel cephalad, and insert a self-retaining retractor.

◧ Total abdominal hysterectomy — operative sequence
1
Open, explore, and gain traction. Assess the pelvis and abdomen; apply clamps across each cornu (catching round ligament, tube and ovarian ligament) to lift and steady the uterus. Upward traction on the uterus opens the pelvic planes and lifts pedicles away from the ureter.
2
Round ligament. Clamp, divide and ligate the round ligament; this opens the anterior and posterior leaves of the broad ligament. This is the "front door" — opening the broad ligament exposes every pedicle to come and lets you find the ureter.
3
Ovarian pedicle — decide first. Removing the ovary (BSO): identify the ureter over the pelvic brim, make a window in the broad ligament, then clamp–divide–ligate the infundibulopelvic ligament (ovarian vessels). Conserving the ovary: clamp–divide–ligate the ovarian ligament and tube close to the uterus instead. The IP sits right beside the ureter at the brim — see the ureter before you clamp.
4
Reflect the bladder. Divide the uterovesical peritoneal fold and dissect the bladder down off the cervix, below the level you intend to open the vagina. Mobilising the bladder moves it — and the lower ureter — out of harm's way before you take the uterine vessels.
5
Skeletonise & clamp the uterine vessels. Clear the areolar tissue to expose the uterine artery and veins at the level of the internal os; double-clamp right against the side of the uterus, divide and securely ligate/transfix. Hugging the uterus keeps you medial to the ureter, which lies ~1.5 cm laterally beneath the artery.
6
Cardinal ligaments. Working down the side of the cervix, take successive clamped–divided–ligated bites of the cardinal ligament, staying close to the cervix (Heaney technique). Close-to-cervix bites protect the ureter as it turns medially toward the bladder.
7
Uterosacral ligaments. Clamp, divide and ligate the uterosacrals posteriorly. This frees the cervix posteriorly and defines the vaginal angles; you may later use these ligaments to support the vault.
8
Open the vagina & remove the uterus. Palpate the cervix to confirm the cervico-vaginal junction; incise the vagina just below the cervix and remove the uterus with the cervix intact. Secure the vaginal angles first — they carry vessels and are a classic bleeding point. Confirming the cervix tip prevents leaving cervix behind or entering the vagina too high/low.
9
Close the vault. Close the vaginal cuff (or leave it open with a haemostatic running stitch, per practice), incorporating the angles and optionally the uterosacrals for support. Ensure it is dry. Angle sutures and vault support reduce bleeding and later vault prolapse.
10
Haemostasis & ureteric check. Inspect every pedicle; confirm ureteric peristalsis/integrity; irrigate and check the field is dry. A deliberate ureter and pedicle check now prevents a re-operation or a missed injury.
11
Close. Confirm swab, instrument and sharps counts; close the abdominal wall in layers (rectus sheath the key strength layer); dress. Document EBL, specimens, and plan. Send the specimen for histology; the count is a hard stop.
The golden rules of hysterectomy

See the ureter before the IP. Hug the uterus for the uterine and cardinal pedicles. Never clamp blindly into blood. These three rules prevent the injuries that define a difficult post-operative course.

Evidence & route

Where the uterus and pathology allow, vaginal or laparoscopic hysterectomy generally offers quicker recovery than open surgery; the abdominal route remains indicated for large uteri, malignancy needing staging, dense adhesions, or when access is otherwise inadequate. Choice of vault closure technique and routine ureteric assessment follow local protocol and case complexity.

Subtotal hysterectomy & the decisions that shape the operation

Two choices — the cervix and the ovaries — define the operation, the risks and the woman's future.

Lesson outcomes

Compare subtotal and total abdominal hysterectomy, and counsel on ovarian conservation versus removal.

Subtotal (supracervical) hysterectomy

The uterine body is removed and the cervix retained. The operation follows the same steps up to and including the uterine vessels; instead of opening the vagina, the cervix is amputated below the level of the uterine vessels and the cervical stump is closed.

Potential advantagesTrade-offs
Shorter, sometimes safer operation when the cervix is difficult to access (dense adhesions, endometriosis, distorted anatomy); avoids the vaginal angle/ureter dissection at the cervixCervix remains → ongoing cervical screening needed; possible cyclical spotting from residual endometrium; does not treat cervical pathology

Trials comparing subtotal and total hysterectomy have not shown a consistent benefit of retaining the cervix for bladder, bowel or sexual function. So the modern indication for subtotal is largely surgical — when removing the cervix would be difficult or dangerous — rather than an assumed functional advantage. Never perform subtotal if there is cervical pathology.

Absolute checks before choosing subtotal

Confirm up-to-date, normal cervical screening and no cervical pathology. Counsel the woman that she must continue cervical screening. If any doubt about cervical or endometrial malignancy, subtotal is contraindicated.

Ovarian conservation vs bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy

  • Conserve — generally preferred in pre-menopausal women without ovarian pathology or high-risk genetics, to avoid surgical menopause and its long-term cardiovascular, bone and cognitive implications.
  • Remove (BSO) — considered for ovarian pathology, high genetic risk (e.g. BRCA — specialist pathway), some malignancies, or after informed discussion in post-menopausal women. Opportunistic salpingectomy (removing the tubes while conserving ovaries) is increasingly offered to reduce future ovarian/tubal cancer risk.
  • If BSO renders a pre-menopausal woman surgically menopausal, discuss HRT unless contraindicated.
Exam pearl

"Subtotal is a surgical decision, not a functional one" and "opportunistic salpingectomy, conserve ovaries in the young" are two modern, high-yield counselling points. Tie every choice back to this individual woman's age, pathology and wishes.

Complications & their management

Most complications are recognised late, not caused. Learn the signs, and recognise them early.

Lesson outcomes

Recognise, prevent and outline management of the major complications of abdominal hysterectomy: haemorrhage, and ureteric, bladder and bowel injury; plus infective, VTE and vault complications.

Haemorrhage

From a pedicle (uterine, IP, cardinal), the vaginal angle, or a broad-ligament ooze. Prevent by secure, close-to-uterus pedicles and a deliberate haemostasis check. If bleeding occurs: pressure and exposure first, identify the source, then re-suture the specific pedicle — do not clamp blindly (ureter). Escalate to internal iliac artery ligation and involve seniors/vascular if uncontrolled.

Ureteric injury

Bladder injury

Commonest at the bladder reflection, especially with adhesions or previous CS. Recognise by seeing the injury, urine in the field, or the catheter balloon. Repair in layers with absorbable suture, test integrity, and leave the catheter for a protected period per protocol.

Usually during adhesiolysis. Small serosal tears may be oversewn; full-thickness injury needs careful repair and, if extensive or contaminated, colorectal input. Recognise a missed injury post-op by ileus, peritonism, fever and deterioration.

  • Vaginal vault complications — vault haematoma/abscess, granulation, and later vault prolapse or (after dehiscence) evisceration — rare but an emergency.
  • Functional — bladder, bowel or sexual dysfunction; and, if BSO, surgical menopause.
Exam pearl

For any "urine leaking after hysterectomy" scenario, think bladder or ureteric fistula, investigate (dye test, imaging, cystoscopy), and involve urology. For "unwell with ileus and fever days later," think bowel injury or ureteric injury/urinoma — do not attribute deterioration to "normal post-op."

Postoperative care, evidence & alternatives

The operation ends at skin closure; recovery is a designed pathway that begins there.

Lesson outcomes

Enhanced recovery after hysterectomy

  • Analgesia — multimodal, opioid-sparing (regional/TAP block + paracetamol + NSAID where safe).
  • Early feeding & mobilisation — reduces ileus and VTE; avoid prolonged bed rest.
  • Catheter — remove early unless a bladder repair dictates otherwise.
  • VTE prophylaxis — mechanical + pharmacological per risk; pelvic surgery is high-risk.
  • Bowel — anticipate transient ileus; escalate if it is prolonged or accompanied by pain/fever (think occult injury).
  • Counselling at discharge — activity, return to work/driving, vaginal bleeding/discharge expectations, when to seek help, HRT if BSO, and continued cervical screening if subtotal.

Where abdominal hysterectomy sits among the alternatives

Before recommending hysterectomy at all, ensure less-invasive options have been considered where appropriate: medical therapy and the levonorgestrel intrauterine system for heavy menstrual bleeding, endometrial ablation, uterine artery embolisation or myomectomy for fibroids (fertility-sparing), and the vaginal or laparoscopic route when hysterectomy is the answer. Reason through planning, technique and a possible complication.

Scenario

A 44-year-old woman has completed her family and has heavy menstrual bleeding with a bulky, fibroid uterus reaching the umbilicus. She is anaemic (Hb 9.2 g/dL). Medical therapy and a levonorgestrel IUS have failed. After counselling she opts for hysterectomy and wishes to keep her ovaries.

1. What route would you plan, and why?

An abdominal hysterectomy is appropriate given the uterine size (umbilical level) limiting vaginal/laparoscopic access. Ovaries will be conserved (pre-menopausal, no ovarian pathology, her wish); consider opportunistic salpingectomy. A midline incision may be needed for a very large uterus, though an experienced surgeon may manage a large uterus through a transverse incision.

2. How would you optimise her before surgery?

Correct the anaemia before an elective operation (iron ± other measures; time permitting), group-and-save/crossmatch given fibroid bulk and bleeding risk, confirm normal cervical screening, and ensure informed consent covering haemorrhage/transfusion, ureteric/bladder injury and VTE. Optimise comorbidities and plan VTE and antibiotic prophylaxis.

3. Which pedicle differs because she is keeping her ovaries, and how?

The ovarian pedicle (step 3). Because the ovary is conserved, you clamp–divide–ligate the ovarian ligament and fallopian tube close to the uterus, rather than the infundibulopelvic ligament. This keeps the ovarian blood supply intact. (If performing opportunistic salpingectomy, the tube is removed while preserving the ovarian vessels.)

4. On day 3 she develops right flank pain, a low-grade fever and a rising creatinine. What is your concern?

A ureteric injury or obstruction (kinking/ligation or partial transection with urinoma) — the fibroid may have distorted the ureter's course, raising risk. Investigate (imaging ± the relevant urological tests), involve urology early, and manage per the injury found (stenting, repair or re-implantation). Do not dismiss it as normal post-operative pain.

Teaching point

Distorted anatomy (large fibroids, broad-ligament fibroids) is exactly when the ureter is displaced and most at risk. The safe operator identifies the ureter early and keeps every pedicle close to the uterus.

References & further reading

Always check for the current version and cross-reference your national and local guidelines.

Important

Guideline numbers, drug regimens and thresholds are updated regularly and vary by country. The list below points you to authoritative sources for the principles taught here — verify the latest edition and follow your own national society and departmental protocols in practice.

Guidelines

  • NICE — Caesarean birth (NG192) and related intrapartum/postnatal guidance.
  • RCOG Green-top Guidelines — e.g. postpartum haemorrhage; placenta praevia and placenta accreta spectrum; reducing the risk of venous thromboembolism in pregnancy/puerperium; peri-operative care.
  • WHO — Surgical Safety Checklist and implementation manual; Robson Classification implementation manual.
  • Your national O&G society / Ministry of Health clinical practice guidelines for caesarean section, hysterectomy, VTE and antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) Society guidelines for caesarean and gynaecologic surgery.

Evidence — key trials & reviews

  • CORONIS Collaborative Group — international factorial trial of caesarean section surgical techniques (The Lancet) and its long-term follow-up.
  • Cochrane systematic reviews of caesarean section techniques (abdominal and uterine entry, closure methods, peritoneal closure, placental delivery).
  • Cochrane and comparative reviews of surgical route for hysterectomy (abdominal vs vaginal vs laparoscopic) and of subtotal vs total hysterectomy.

Textbooks (operative technique)

  • Munro Kerr's Operative Obstetrics.
  • Te Linde's Operative Gynecology.
  • Bonney's Gynaecological Surgery / Shaw's Textbook of Operative Gynaecology.
  • A standard postgraduate O&G text (e.g. Dewhurst's Textbook of Obstetrics & Gynaecology) for background.

eLearning & simulation

  • RCOG StratOG and the eLearning for Healthcare O&G programmes.
  • Local skills-lab and simulation curricula for surgical steps, suturing and emergencies (PPH drills).
